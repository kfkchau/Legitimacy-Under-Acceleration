# Hormuz Waters: The Law of the Sea Under Acceleration

> **Talks as rule-change events: universality, neutrality, and precedent containment**

Governance is a rule-management function: how rules are made, updated, communicated, implemented, enforced, and kept legitimate as reality changes. It’s a civilisation technology, and like any technology it can fall out of date. This isn’t only true for domestic laws and policies. It’s true for international rule systems too—such as the Law of the Sea.

Before anything else: this isn’t a blame piece, and it isn’t trying to adjudicate who is “right.” It’s a design diagnosis about rule quality—whether the law-of-the-sea rule system can still coordinate predictable, legitimate behavior when the operating environment is changing fast.

When the combined tempo of technology and human activity outpaces a rule system’s scope and manageability, governance debt accumulates: patches pile up, exceptions multiply, and the lived operating rules drift away from the formal text. The Strait crisis matters because it makes this failure mode visible. Rule modernisation isn’t an academic preference; it’s what keeps coordination legitimate under acceleration.

Whatever the agreement contains, it will either:

(a) reinforce a universal standard, or

(b) create a high-salience exception that can leak precedent.

This is less about the parties and more about what kind of rule-change event the world is witnessing. The question isn’t whether a deal restores calm. It’s whether it restores a rule that can scale.

Right now, the Strait problem is being negotiated in a world where the law-of-the-sea rule system is running into a familiar failure mode: the operating environment has accelerated, and the rule system is struggling to coordinate reality at tempo.

By “accelerated,” I mean capability shifts that change what distance, presence, and verification mean in practice—longer-range strike and sensing, plus electronic warfare (EW) that degrades navigation and situational clarity.

That’s not a moral judgement. It’s a design diagnosis.

## Why this is a rule-quality story, not an enforcement story

When people hear “the Law of the Sea is failing,” they often jump straight to enforcement: who will escort, who will punish, who will back down.

That’s not the lens here.

Rule effectiveness is about whether the rule system functions as a coordination standard—whether diverse actors can predict what’s allowed, what’s expected, and what will happen next, without bespoke bargaining each time the environment shifts.

Enforcement matters, but it’s downstream. A rule can be enforceable and still be low-quality—unclear, brittle, non-scalable, or easily laundered into discretionary control. And in a chokepoint, when the environment is hazardous and the fact layer is degraded, rule quality becomes visible fast.

## The rule-suitability problem is twofold

First: **security geometry**. The distance at which states feel safe is a moving function of weapons and sensing, while the law encodes largely fixed spatial lines. Historically, maritime distance lines reflected older capability assumptions. Those assumptions fit the operating environment of their time; the environment has changed again, while the update path for core spatial logic remains slow and politically hard.

Territorial dominion ends where the range of weapons ends **[terrae dominum finitur, ubi finitur armorium vis]**. The point is not the Latin. The point is that boundary-thinking has long depended on “reach,” and “reach” changes with technology.

Second: **system geometry**. Ocean resources and uses are coupled—one layered, continuous system. Fish stocks migrate, ecosystems spill across boundaries, and externalities propagate. When governance “slices” a coupled system as if it were separable, it pushes conflicts and coordination problems into the gaps, producing governance externalities and recurrent contestation.

If you don’t name these two geometries, chokepoint “solutions” keep collapsing into improvised control interfaces: prior-permission requirements, corridors, selective exclusions, fees, “coordination requirements,” and safety language that quietly becomes gatekeeping.

Those patches can restore partial function. They can also teach the world a new operating rule that sits outside the formal rule system.

## Which brings us to the talks

Peace talks are not just de-escalation events. They are rule-change events.

I don’t expect these talks to end with a formal UNCLOS update. So what we’re watching is almost certainly a patchy fix outside the rule system.

That’s not automatically bad. Patches are sometimes necessary. The danger is when a patch becomes precedent—when it hardens into “how chokepoints work now,” without the legitimacy and constraints of a universal rule system.

So instead of arguing about who is right, the serious question is: what happens to the rule system when this patch lands?

To keep this clean, you can judge any outcome—any text, any mechanism—through three rule-integrity tests:

1) **Universality (including the crisis test)**  
2) **Neutrality (auditability)**  
3) **Precedent containment**

If the outcome fails these, it may still “work” locally. But it will degrade the rule system globally.

## Test 1: Universality (including the crisis test)

Universality isn’t a slogan. It’s a property: one standard that applies to all similarly situated users.

In chokepoints, the universality failure mode is simple: access based on identity, affiliation, flag, cargo, or political alignment. “Commercial ships can pass, but not if they are associated with X.” “You can transit, but only with our prior permission.” “You can pass, but only under our escort, under our rules, under our classification of what counts as transit.”

You can dress that up as safety, coordination, or deconfliction. But if the operating rule becomes permission-based access, universality has already been compromised.

The crisis test is the part people forget: a rule that only works in calm weather is not a chokepoint rule. Chokepoints are where hazard, uncertainty, degraded visibility, and jittery insurance are normal. A “universal” rule that collapses under stress isn’t universal in practice—it’s conditional on the environment being nice.

That’s why “we reopened the Strait” isn’t the metric. The metric is whether the system has restored a scalable coordination standard under the actual conditions that broke it.

## Test 2: Neutrality (auditability)

Neutrality is the ability to sustain legitimacy without requiring the world to trust anyone’s narrative.

In a stable environment, neutrality is easier: events are observable, evidence is plentiful, claims are contestable in public.

In an accelerated environment—EW, spoofing, disrupted signals, restricted imagery, misinformation—neutrality becomes the constraint. The fact layer degrades. When the fact layer degrades, rule legitimacy either rests on independent auditability, or it collapses into “who do you believe?”

Neutrality is not “being fair-minded.” It’s being auditable: can third parties verify the basics—who transited, under what conditions, what restrictions were applied, whether incidents happened—without becoming parties to narrative war?

If the mechanism depends on one actor’s discretionary classification (“unauthorised transit,” “hostile,” “safe,” “not safe”), and there’s no credible neutral audit layer, neutrality fails. The rule becomes an instrument, not a standard.

That doesn’t just create disagreement. It changes behavior: operators route away, insurers price out risk, and “rights” stop producing throughput.

## Test 3: Precedent containment

Precedent containment is the difference between a patch and a silent rewrite of the rule system.

If the Strait reopens via an exception that others can copy, we haven’t solved the chokepoint problem—we’ve taught the world a new way to close chokepoints.

That’s the core risk of crisis patches outside the rule system: they are high-salience. They’re watched. They’re imitated. They become a template.

Containment is not achieved by saying “this is unique.” It’s achieved by design constraints:

- **bounded scope** (what exactly this applies to, and what it does not)
- **explicit sunsets** (when it ends)
- **clear triggers for re-entry** (when it must be pulled back into formal multilateral rule machinery)
- **a shape that cannot be cheaply repurposed** into tolling, permission-based transit, or discretionary exclusion elsewhere

Without those containment controls, you don’t have a patch. You have precedent in waiting.

## Patch outside the rule system vs upgrade of the rule system

It helps to name the two pathways plainly.

### Path 1: Patch outside the rule system (exception drift)

A patch can restore partial function quickly—reduced incidents, some resumption of traffic, a corridor, an agreed coordination channel.

But if it is permission-shaped, non-auditable, or precedent-leaky, it carries a long-run cost: it normalises discretionary control as the operating rule. That cost shows up as persistent routing risk, higher insurance and freight pricing, and a wider incentive for other chokepoints to experiment with tolls, permissions, or selective exclusion.

### Path 2: Upgrade the rule system (system repair)

An upgrade is different: it changes the shared rule system, or at least builds mechanisms that clearly point back toward it—making future chokepoint crises more governable under universal, auditable, bounded standards.

If the Strait reopens via a mechanism that is universal, auditable, and contained, the crisis becomes a forcing function for repairing rule legitimacy under acceleration rather than eroding it.

## Apply the framework to the obvious candidates

This is not about picking winners. It’s about seeing what each option teaches the world.

### 1) Permission-based corridor / “coordination requirements”

**Universality:** fails if access depends on identity, affiliation, or discretionary “authorisation.”  
**Neutrality:** fails if corridor use depends on one actor’s classification without independent audit.  
**Precedent containment:** fails if it looks like a reusable template for controlling other straits.

This can still restore some flow. But it risks teaching a new normal: “transit is conditional and permissioned under crisis.”

### 2) Toll / fee logic

**Universality:** fails if pricing is discriminatory or tied to political vetting.  
**Neutrality:** fails if compliance and exemptions are not independently auditable.  
**Precedent containment:** high risk—because a toll is easy to copy.

Even if it “works,” toll logic is a powerful precedent: it turns a navigation norm into a monetisable control interface.

### 3) Escort / deconfliction channel

**Universality:** can pass if it is offered on neutral terms to similarly situated traffic.  
**Neutrality:** can pass if it is paired with an audit layer (who transited, what rules applied, what incidents occurred).  
**Precedent containment:** can pass if it is explicitly bounded and sunsetted, and clearly framed as a temporary safety measure rather than a new permission gate.

This can be a responsible patch—if it is designed to be a bridge back to the rule system, not a replacement for it.

## Why this can’t be fixed with case-by-case bargains alone

The temptation is obvious: treat each chokepoint as a special case. Negotiate a local workaround. Move on.

But chokepoints are not isolated. The same structural pattern shows up across theatres—different geographies, similar failure modes—because it’s a rule–environment mismatch problem, not a one-off local quirk. They are nodes in a global network. Rule changes at nodes propagate—through precedent, through insurer models, through operator behavior, through expectations about what “normal” looks like under stress.

A case-by-case mindset is how you accumulate governance debt in global form: patches pile up, exceptions multiply, and the universal rule system becomes a thin layer of rhetoric sitting above an operational reality of permissions and discretionary control.

If you care about legitimacy, the goal is not “no patches.” The goal is patches that either (a) preserve a universal standard, or (b) are explicitly contained and routed back into a legitimate upgrade pathway.

## The deeper point: the rule system is outpaced, and the world improvises

Acceleration doesn’t just raise the stakes. It changes the geometry of the problem.

Security geometry moves because arm length moves.

System geometry is coupled because the ocean is not a set of neat parcels; it’s a continuous, layered system with externalities.

So a static, land-slice sovereignty mindset will keep producing recurrent chokepoint stress, recurring contestation over resources, and recurring incentives to “solve” through control interfaces.

I’ve been making a version of this chokepoint-legitimacy argument since 2018.

That is why the talks matter beyond the Strait. Not because they decide one conflict, but because they decide—implicitly—what kind of rule-change event this is going to be: a patch that leaks precedent, or a forcing function for repairing rule legitimacy under acceleration.
